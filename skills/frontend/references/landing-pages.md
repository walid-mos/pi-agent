# Landing pages

What a page that asks a visitor to act shows and says. Distilled from the tips of https://www.uidesign.tips/ux-tips,
which the user brought in as our practice. How each element looks stays the project's design system's.

The tips left out (humour, a mascot, easter eggs, filling the page from the visitor's location or name) are a brand's
choice: make them only when the user asks for them, never as a default.

## Proof

- Show the product, never stock imagery or an abstract illustration: real screenshots, or a mockup of the actual
  interface the visitor can click through, because a visitor believes what they see the product do.
- Show the change the product makes: the task, how it is done today and what goes wrong, then the same task with the
  product, in images or video rather than in prose.
- Give exact numbers ("214 projects delivered", not "200+"): a round figure reads as invented. Where the gain depends
  on the visitor (time or money saved), a calculator working from their own inputs proves more than any claim.
- Social proof comes from people the visitor takes as peers or authorities: client logos, testimonials with a name, a
  role and a face. A face turned toward the headline or the call to action leads the eye there.
- Never invent a testimonial, a logo, a client, a number or a face, since the page would lie in the client's name:
  leave a placeholder that says what is missing, and list the missing items in your report.

## Calls to action

- A call to action is a verb and what the visitor gets ("Book my audit", "Create the first board"), never a label
  that fits any page ("Submit", "Learn more", "Get started" alone).
- The header's call to action follows the reader: signing up for a visitor, the dashboard for a signed-in user.
- The last section before the footer repeats the main call to action beside a preview of the product.
- A call to action is never red: red is kept for destructive or irreversible actions (delete, downgrade, revoke), and
  spent anywhere else it stops warning.

## Sign-up, login and onboarding

- The strongest proof (a testimonial, the core value) sits beside the sign-up form. The login screen, seen at every
  visit, restates the value or shows what is new instead of standing empty.
- When the product offers sign-in with a provider (Google, GitHub), that comes first; email and password stays below
  it for those who will not link an account.
- A permission request says, before the system prompt appears, what the product will do with the access, and comes
  at the moment the feature needs it, never in a batch at sign-up.
- Where competitors hold the visitor's data, onboarding offers to import it from them: fear of losing that data is
  what keeps people where they are.
- At an upgrade prompt, a paid feature may show a blurred or locked preview of itself, so the visitor sees what they
  would get.

## Pricing

- Each plan names who it is for (someone working alone, a small team, a company) and carries its own icon or
  illustration, so the visitor finds their plan before reading the prices.
